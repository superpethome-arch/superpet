Why Does My Dog Need Regular Nail Trims?

Overgrown nails are one of the most commonly overlooked aspects of dog care. Nails that are too long cause real, measurable problems over time:

  • Pain and discomfort during walking. When nails touch the ground with every step, they push back into the nail bed, creating chronic low-grade pain. This is especially pronounced on hard floors and pavement.
  • Toe and paw posture problems. Long nails change how a dog distributes weight across its paws. Over months and years, this can lead to misaligned toes and altered gait mechanics that affect joints higher up the leg.
  • Snagging and tearing. Long nails, especially dewclaws, can catch on carpet, grass, or furniture and tear partially or fully. A torn nail is painful and sometimes requires veterinary treatment.

Many Cape Cod dog owners assume that outdoor activity keeps nails worn down naturally. Sandy beaches, grass, and soft ground surfaces do very little to file nails. Only concrete and asphalt provide meaningful wear. Even very active dogs that spend time at the beach or on trails typically still need regular professional trims.

The general guideline for most dogs is a nail trim every 4 to 6 weeks. Dogs with faster nail growth or less pavement time may need trims more frequently.

Signs Your Dog's Nails Are Too Long

You do not need a groomer to tell you when nails are overdue. These are the clearest signs to watch for:

  • Clicking on hard floors. If you can hear your dog walking on tile, hardwood, or linoleum, the nails are long enough to be making contact with the ground. That contact is a sign they should have been trimmed already.
  • Nails visibly curl or extend past the paw pad. When viewed from the side, properly trimmed nails should not touch the ground when the dog is standing. If they do, or if they curve, they need to be cut.
  • Uncomfortable walking posture. A dog that appears to be walking on the back of its paws, shifts its weight oddly, or avoids certain surfaces may be compensating for nail discomfort.
  • Nails snagging on fabric or carpet. If nails catch when the dog walks across a rug, they are likely too long and may be at risk of tearing.

For dogs with dark nails, such as black Labrador Retrievers and many mixed breeds, it is harder to see where the nail ends and the quick (the live blood vessel inside the nail) begins. This makes professional trimming especially worthwhile. Sabina has experience trimming dark nails safely, using technique and knowledge of anatomy rather than guesswork.

Does Super Pet Handle Nervous or Difficult Dogs for Nail Trims?

Yes. Many dogs that are well-behaved in other settings become anxious or reactive specifically around nail trims. This is common and does not mean a dog is aggressive or unmanageable. It usually reflects a past experience of having the quick accidentally cut, or simply the unfamiliar sensation of having paws handled.

Sabina is trained and experienced in working with anxious and reactive dogs. She adjusts her pace, grip, and positioning to reduce the stress of the process. Super Pet's one-on-one, no-cage approach helps significantly. Dogs are not left waiting in a crate between steps, which reduces overall stress levels during the appointment.

If your dog has had a very difficult experience with nail trims at another location, mention it when calling to book. The team can note the history and plan the appointment accordingly.

For dogs with extreme nail-trim anxiety, sedation is not something Super Pet offers. If your dog's reaction is severe enough that sedation has been discussed with a veterinarian, consult your vet first and then contact Super Pet to discuss options before booking.

For most nervous dogs, pairing the nail trim with a full groom or spa treatment at the same appointment creates a more positive overall experience. A longer, calmer session with a skilled groomer is often more effective than a quick standalone trim for a dog that needs time to settle.
